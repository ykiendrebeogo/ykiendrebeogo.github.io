The Poor Pay Twice: Why Standard Rate Rules Get Energy Shocks Wrong

Created on November 25, 2026 • 5 min read

2026   ·   monetary policy   energy shocks   inequality   HANK   central banks

When energy prices surge, low-income households are hit hardest — and aggressive rate hikes compound the damage. New research shows the Fed and ECB were right to hold back in 2022, and derives the optimal rule that explains why.

On February 24, 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Within four months, European natural gas prices had risen more than 300 percent, global oil benchmarks had surpassed $120 per barrel, and headline inflation in the United States had reached 9.0 percent — the highest in four decades. Central banks faced an immediate and painful choice: raise rates aggressively to prevent a wage-price spiral, or accept temporarily elevated inflation to shield households already squeezed by the energy price surge?

The Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank ultimately raised rates, but substantially less than a standard New Keynesian Taylor rule would have prescribed. Critics called it insufficient. In this paper, I argue the critics had it backwards — and provide a formal model that explains why going slower was not a mistake but the optimal response.

Energy expenditure is regressive — and that changes everything

The core insight is simple but its implications are far-reaching: energy is not a neutral consumption good. The numbers are striking. Bottom-quintile households devote 11.4 percent of their budgets to energy in the US, versus just 5.0 percent for top-quintile households. The gap is even wider in the Euro Area: 11.3 against 4.1 percent. An energy price spike therefore acts as a regressive lump-sum tax on the household sector, falling disproportionately on those least able to absorb it.

Standard Representative-Agent New Keynesian (RANK) models are blind to this. By construction, they assume a single representative household and therefore cannot capture the distributional asymmetry of an energy shock. The optimal policy they prescribe — aggressive tightening — is calibrated to protect the average household. But there is no average household in an energy crisis.

A Ramsey-optimal rule with a distributional correction

I embed this distributional fact into a Heterogeneous-Agent New Keynesian (HANK) model and derive the Ramsey-optimal monetary policy response. The result is a closed-form optimality condition — the HANK Ramsey rule. It modifies the standard Taylor prescription by subtracting a distributional correction tied to two observable quantities: the share of hand-to-mouth consumers in the economy, and the income gradient of energy expenditure shares.

The intuition is sharp. When the central bank raises interest rates in response to an energy shock, it compounds the distributional harm through a second regressive channel: tighter credit hurts borrowing-constrained low-income households the most. A Ramsey-optimal central bank internalizes both channels and therefore prescribes a systematically less aggressive tightening than its representative-agent counterpart.

Crucially, the correction differs across economies in a testable way. The Euro Area’s income gradient (Q1/Q5 = 2.76) is steeper than the US’s (Q1/Q5 = 2.28). My model therefore predicts the ECB’s optimal rate should sit further below the RANK benchmark than the Fed’s — by a specific, model-derived ratio of 1.312.

The 2022 episode confirms the theory

My model’s predictions are remarkably precise. Both the Fed and the ECB set rates below their respective RANK benchmarks throughout the 2022–2023 tightening cycle. At twelve months after shock onset, Taylor rule residuals were −2.28 percentage points for the Fed and −2.99 percentage points for the ECB. The cross-country residual ratio — the ECB’s shortfall relative to the Fed’s — was 1.309, within 0.003 of the structural prediction of 1.312 derived entirely from household survey moments. That a theoretical ratio calibrated on distributional data lines up this closely with observed central bank behavior is not coincidental: it suggests both institutions were implicitly responding to the distributional regressivity of the shock.

Who gains under optimal policy?

A full Bewley–Huggett–Aiyagari welfare analysis quantifies what was at stake. I find consumption-equivalent welfare gaps of 0.81 percentage points in the United States and 1.36 percentage points in the Euro Area between observed policy and the RANK benchmark. The bottom two income quintiles in the Euro Area register absolute welfare gains under my optimal policy — not merely smaller losses, but genuine gains. This is progressive redistribution of a kind that standard representative-agent frameworks cannot even conceive of, let alone prescribe.

What this means for central banks

My conclusion is pointed. Standard Taylor rules are not neutral instruments during commodity shocks: in heterogeneous-agent economies, they systematically over-prescribe tightening and impose a welfare cost that falls heaviest on the households with the least capacity to bear it. The distributional correction derived here is not a departure from optimality — it is the optimal rule, once the economy is modeled honestly.

For central banks navigating future energy shocks, the message is practical: the income gradient of energy expenditure shares is a measurable, survey-based statistic that should enter the policy calculus alongside the standard inflation and output-gap terms. Ignoring it is not conservative. It is costly.

This post summarizes the working paper “Optimal Monetary Policy Response to Energy Price Shocks: Evidence from the 2022 Russia–Ukraine Episode” by Youssouf Kiendrebeogo (Working Paper, November 2026).
