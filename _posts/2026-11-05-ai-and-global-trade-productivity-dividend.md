***Standard AI productivity estimates treat countries as closed economies. New research across 77 economies shows this assumption is not conservative — it is structurally wrong.***

Artificial intelligence is raising productivity around the world — but not uniformly, and not for the reasons most analyses suggest. Standard estimates of AI's economic impact treat countries as closed economies. New research shows this assumption is not conservative; it is structurally wrong.

Using a general equilibrium model calibrated to 77 economies and the OECD Inter-Country Input-Output tables, the analysis traces AI productivity gains through global supply chains. The finding is stark: open-economy channels amplify advanced-economy gains by a factor of 2.7 relative to the closed-economy benchmark. Roughly half of the United States' AI-driven labor productivity gain originates abroad, flowing in through cheaper imported intermediates as AI reduces costs across its supplier network. A model that ignores trade systematically misattributes both the source and the scale of these gains.

## The divergence challenge for emerging markets

Advanced economies gain an estimated 2.40 percentage points per year in labor productivity; emerging markets gain 1.07 pp/yr; developing economies 0.59 pp/yr. The 1.33 pp/yr gap is not incidental — it reflects three mutually reinforcing structural disadvantages: lower AI adoption rates, less dense domestic supply chains, and more peripheral positions in global value chains. All three forces amplify the same technology shock differently.

Critically, domestic AI policy alone cannot close this gap: a country that fully closes its adoption shortfall still cannot recover gains that flow through supply-chain linkages it does not possess.

## A free lever that is being underused

Reducing barriers to AI-intensive services trade — through WTO negotiations or bilateral digital economy agreements — adds 0.122 pp/yr for advanced and 0.055 pp/yr for emerging economies at zero additional technology cost. Every economy in the sample registers a positive net gain from open AI trade; the supply-chain transmission channel dominates the terms-of-trade cost everywhere.

Digital trade liberalization is, in this framework, a Pareto improvement that disproportionately benefits the countries least able to generate AI gains domestically. The implication for Fund surveillance and member country advice is direct: trade integration and digital openness are not ancillary to AI policy — they are its primary amplification mechanism. Closed-economy analyses of AI will continue to understate what is at stake.
